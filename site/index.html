The problem: nameplates that physics does not allow

Online marketplaces sell small turbines rated "3000 W" or "6000 W" with rotors half a metre wide. The power a rotor can extract is fixed by its swept area and by the wind, not by the label.

Declared

6000 W

VAWT kit, rotor 0.53 m x 0.75 m (0.40 m²)

Physical limit at 10 m/s

144 W

Betz limit: 59.3% of the 243 W carried by the wind through that rotor

Realistic at 10 m/s

~68 W

How it works

Your anemometer

Point WhatIfWind at the wind sensor you already have in Home Assistant. It captures what grid weather data cannot: your height, your obstacles, your terrain.

Your turbine

Enter the rotor geometry from the datasheet, or the manufacturer's power curve. The nameplate is only used as a cap.

Real numbers

Live power, simulated energy, annual projection, capacity factor and a daily/monthly/yearly series, backfilled from InfluxDB if you have it.

A real case: two years on a rooftop

Anemometer on a roof at about 6.5 m, two years of hourly data (Nov 2024 to Oct 2026). Mean wind 0.74 m/s; only 2.6% of the hours averaged above 3 m/s.

Your site is a data point

Small wind is full of promises and short on measured results. If you run WhatIfWind, share what you found in Show and tell: a short form, no exact address needed.

  • region and type of site, anemometer and its height;
  • mean wind and the period analysed;
  • turbines evaluated, simulated kWh per year, and what you decided;
  • if you installed one: the real production against the prediction.
